It tells you when you're wrong.

NADNAPS builds a clean, point-in-time database from NSE's own public files on your computer, then tests trading rules against random baselines. A rule counts as an edge only if it beats random picks on data it was not designed on.

The honest answer, so far

Every famous swing-trading rule tested in NADNAPS so far did no better than random picks or simply holding the market. That's not a bug. It's the point: a backtest only means something once it's compared with random picks.

Did I fool myself?

Test periods are fixed in advance, validation looks are counted and limited, and a registered rule can't be quietly changed afterwards. One screen exists just to ask whether your best result is luck.

Write a rule in plain text

$ nadnaps rule check "close > sma(200) and rsi(14) < 35"
$ nadnaps research my_rule train # 2012–2017, against random picks
$ nadnaps stress my_rule # rerun at 1.5x and 2x costs

Your own rules

MACD, Bollinger, ADX, stochastics, delivery %, Nifty, market breadth, weekly bars. Rank buys, set targets and stops.

Against random portfolios

Every run is compared with random picks using the same exits, sizing and costs.

Real costs

STT, stamp duty, exchange fees, DP charges, slippage. Re-price any run at Zerodha, Dhan, Groww, Angel One or Upstox charges.

Reports you can open

Verdict, charts, every trade, results by market regime, tax estimate by financial year.

Stays on your computer

Nothing about your lists, rules or results is ever sent anywhere.

What you need

  • Linux (Windows and macOS are not tested yet)
  • Python 3.12, 3.13 or 3.14, installed with pipx
  • About 4 GB of RAM, and about 8 GB of disk for the first setup (about 3 GB afterwards)
  • Internet for NSE's public files: history since 2010 on first setup, then daily updates
